Yes — this is one of the highest-leverage things you can do.
If you gather a large amount of high-quality tactical data and install it as persistent “memory” inside Reverse Picks, the app can become meaningfully smarter without having to call Grok or Gemini for every single explanation.
What this actually means
You’re building a knowledge layer (or memory system) that the app owns. When it needs to write a tactical explanation, it first retrieves the most relevant pieces of that knowledge and only then (optionally) uses an LLM to turn them into fluent text.
This is the standard modern pattern: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) + structured memory.
What kind of tactical data is most valuable
Prioritize these categories:
1.  Player Role Profiles
	•  Ball-playing CB vs pure stopper
	•  Progressive full-back vs defensive full-back
	•  High-volume 6 vs destroyer 6
	•  Inverted winger vs traditional winger
	•  Typical pass volume, progressive pass %, touches by zone for each archetype
2.  Team Style Vectors
	•  Possession % + directness
	•  Build-up style (short, progressive, long)
	•  Press intensity / PPDA
	•  Defensive line height
	•  How they behave when leading vs trailing
	•  Home vs away differences
3.  Matchup Dynamics
	•  What happens when a high-possession team faces a low-block
	•  Ball-playing CBs vs high press
	•  Full-backs vs aggressive wingers
	•  Expected effect on pass volume, carries, progressive actions, etc.
4.  Contextual Rules / Heuristics
	•  “When expected possession is under 45%, even high-volume CBs usually drop 8–15% in pass attempts”
	•  “In low-scoring scripts, progressive pass volume often stays high even if total passes fall”
	•  Common failure modes for certain props
5.  Historical patterns specific to your edges
	•  Situations where your model’s projections have been most accurate or have systematically over/under-shot
How to install it as “memory”
You have a few practical options, from simplest to more powerful:
A. Structured database + retrieval (recommended starting point)
Store the data in a normal database (Postgres, etc.) with good tagging. When generating a write-up, query the relevant rows and inject them into the prompt. Simple, controllable, and very effective.
B. Vector store / embeddings (classic RAG)
Convert tactical notes, role descriptions, team reports, etc. into embeddings. At runtime, retrieve the top most similar chunks to the current player + match context. This handles more free-form knowledge well.
C. Knowledge graph
Link entities (Player → Role → Typical Stats → Team Style → Opponent Style). More powerful for complex reasoning, but heavier to build and maintain.
D. Hybrid (best long-term)
Structured fields for the numbers + vector memory for the qualitative tactical explanations + light knowledge graph for relationships.
Realistic impact
•  The app will produce much more consistent and grounded write-ups.
•  You can dramatically reduce how often you need to call expensive models (or use smaller/cheaper ones).
•  Explanations become more “yours” — they reflect the tactical framework you believe in rather than whatever the general LLM happens to say that day.
•  You still usually want a language model at the final step to turn the retrieved knowledge into clean prose. Pure retrieval without any generation tends to feel robotic.
Important limitations
•  Garbage in = garbage out. The quality of the tactical data you feed it matters more than the volume.
•  It won’t magically invent new deep insights the data doesn’t support.
•  You still need good retrieval logic (what to pull for a given player/prop/matchup).
•  Maintenance is real work — styles and roles evolve.
